Four surfaces, one API, two domains.

Landing page, web app, Android now and iOS later, plus a staff console that deliberately does not live on the same registrable domain as anything a customer touches. This is the shape to build against once the wholesale contract is signed.

The boundary that matters

Why not admin.bilbymobile.com. Because cookies scope to a registrable domain, not a subdomain. A single cross site scripting bug anywhere in the customer web app would put a staff session in reach, and a session with refund and costing rights is the one session worth stealing. Moving the console to nextwave.au costs a separate login system and buys the guarantee that customer code and staff code never share a cookie jar.

Two rules, enforced in middleware, both failing closed: nothing customer facing is served from the admin host, and nothing on the admin host is reachable without a staff session.

Two domains, two jobs

bilbymobile.com is the brand and the settled apex: it is the name on the Play listing, the name a person says out loud, and the name every customer host hangs off. nextwave.au is the business behind it and carries nothing a customer touches, which is what makes it the right place for the staff console and the right address on the footer for anyone who needs the company rather than the support queue.

The checkout handoff, and the one non obvious bug in it

The mobile app hands checkout to a real browser, which is a different origin carrying no session. A checkout link with no identity lands the payment on a fresh anonymous user, and the customer never sees what they bought. The fix is a signed single use token on the URL with a short expiry, exchanged once at the checkout origin. Not a shared cookie domain, which would undo the isolation the host split exists to buy.

Every mutation writes an audit row: actor, role, action, target, before, after, address, timestamp. The log is append only and no role can delete from it, owner included. That is the point of an audit log and it is worth being inflexible about, because the first instinct after a mistake is always to tidy it away.

Many suppliers, one interface

Signing a wholesaler is a configuration change, and so is signing the next four. The design separates what you sell from who delivers it. A catalogue item is a thing with a price and a description; a fulfiller is whoever provisions it. An eSIM plan happens to be a catalogue item fulfilled by a wholesale eSIM provider, and nothing in that sentence is specific to eSIMs, which is what makes a second product category cheap rather than a rewrite.

interface Fulfiller {
  readonly id: string;              // "esimaccess", "airalo", "resellportal"
  readonly kind: "esim" | string;   // the category, so a future product is not a special case
  health(): Promise<Health>;        // polled, and surfaced in the console
  balance(): Promise<Money | null>; // prepaid wallet, where the provider has one
  quote(item: CatalogItem): Promise<Quote>;
  fulfil(order: Order): Promise<Fulfilment>;
}

Routing lives in the database, not in code. One row per country naming a preferred and a fallback provider. Changing who serves Indonesia is a click in the console rather than a deploy, which matters the first time a provider has an outage on a Friday night.

Cost is captured on the order, never looked up. The rate paid on the day is a fact about that order. Recomputing margin from today's rate card makes last month's numbers move, and a dashboard whose history changes is one nobody trusts twice.

A provider failing health checks stops receiving orders automatically, and the console says so, rather than silently failing purchases until somebody notices. No supplier response reaches a customer verbatim either: their error strings are written for a reseller integrator, not for a person standing at an airport.

iOS, later

The app is Flutter, so iOS is the same Dart against the same API, and the design tokens carry across untouched. Two things are genuinely iOS only. Install uses a universal link into the carrier entitlement flow rather than a QR scan, which is a better experience and a different code path. And the payment question needs answering before any iOS work starts: Apple requires in app purchase for digital goods consumed in the app, whether a mobile data plan counts is not obvious, and the pattern the incumbents use is checkout in an external browser. Getting it wrong is a rejection rather than a warning, and if in app purchase turns out to be required then Apple's commission belongs inside the pricing model rather than discovered after launch.

What can stop you, in order

  1. Carriage service provider registrationUnder the 2025 telecommunications amendments, wholesale providers are prohibited from supplying an unregistered carriage service provider. This gates supply itself, not just launch, so it sits ahead of everything commercial.
  2. Payment provider underwritingTravel and telecommunications are both elevated risk categories for acquirers, and a new business in either can wait weeks for a decision or be declined outright. Start it in parallel with the supplier contract, because it blocks revenue completely and nothing about the code changes the answer.
  3. Apple's payment rulesAnswer before any iOS effort is spent, because the answer changes the pricing model rather than the code.
  4. Written only support under the TCP CodeNo telephone channel is a defensible product decision. Confirm it survives registration, because the obligation attaches then rather than at launch.
  5. The numbers on the landing page190 plus countries and 200 plus carriers are representations about a future matter until a supplier is signed, and the burden of showing reasonable grounds sits with you, not with the person who reads it.
