Brand mark · four directions for decision

Keep the bilby. Lose the cape.

A bilby is a genuinely good asset, because the animal's defining feature is enormous ears and ears are antennae. That is a real bridge between the animal and the product, and it is already written into your design system. A caped superhero is a different bridge, to a different product.

What I would keep and what I would drop

Keep from the mockup

  • The bilby itself. Distinctive, Australian, ownable, and nobody in this category has an animal.
  • A phone mockup in the hero. People buy an app; showing it is right.
  • Destination search as the main action. That is the actual first job.
  • Scroll reveals and a moving background. Restrained motion earns its place.

Drop, and why

  • The cape and mask. Your buyer is anxious about being cut off abroad. A cartoon superhero is the wrong register at the moment that matters.
  • Indigo to cyan gradient. That is Tailwind's default indigo 500 to cyan 500, the single most recognisable generated look on the web.
  • Space Grotesk. Same problem: it reads as the default choice.
  • A trusted by logo row. You have no customers yet. Do not build a section that needs inventing.
  • A second palette entirely. The Flutter app is already built against the existing tokens. Changing them means rebuilding it.

The palette question, side by side

Proposed in the mockup

Indigo, cyan, amber

6366f1 06b6d4 f59e0b 0f172a

The problem is not that it is ugly. Those are Tailwind's indigo 500, cyan 500, amber 500 and slate 900, unmodified. Three brand colours also means no colour carries meaning, and it would require rebuilding the Flutter app, which is written against the existing tokens.

Already in the codebase

One accent, four tonal surfaces

0E9C86 2BE8C8 F4F8F8 07080B

Two steps of one hue, deep teal for light backgrounds and luminous aqua for dark, plus the two grounds. Amber and red stay reserved for warnings and errors so they still mean something when they appear.

My recommendation is Direction A, Signal Ears. It is the only mark that says both things at once: ears, and signal strength. It holds at 16 pixels, it works as a loading indicator when the bars fill one by one, and it gives you an animation that is about connecting rather than about a cartoon flying.

Direction B is the safe second. More abstract, calmer, and it would scale down further than anything else here. Choose it if you want the brand to feel like infrastructure rather than like a character.

Direction D is the one to resist unless you are certain. A literal animal is charming in a pitch and it drifts toward cute in production, which is the same trap as the cape.
